Design review · investment diligence

Béa — design & experience

A review of the interface, its mechanics and the system underneath, for an investment decision. Read alongside the engineering assessment: this one is about whether people can use the thing, and whether a design team could grow it.

Commit b7d30f2Version 8.4.36 tabs · 21 routes 17 Sep 2026

Verdict

Strong voice. Weak system.

Béa has the thing that cannot be bought late: a real point of view. The positioning is written down and the product actually obeys it, the copy has a consistent character across every screen, and the colour work is more sophisticated than the stage would suggest. That is the expensive half of design, and it is done.

What is missing is the cheap half — the system that makes the voice repeatable. Twenty-six ad-hoc type sizes, five overlapping surface treatments, four different ways to delete something, and a second unused component library sitting in the repository. None of that is visible to users today because one person holds the whole design in their head. It becomes visible the moment a second designer joins.

Recommendation: the design is an asset, not a liability — but budget one quarter of systems work before hiring into it, or the first two hires will spend their time producing inconsistency.

Findings · high

What would cost you

D1

The interface needs 1,464 lines to explain itself

Usability
Evidence
Three separate onboarding systems ship in the app: a replay tour (444 lines), a per-screen guide (515), a spotlight overlay (116), plus 389 lines of step definitions and state. They drive 55 guide steps against 58 anchor points planted through the UI. On top of that, trips.tsx alone carries 42 separate blocks of muted explanatory text.
Reading
An interface that requires this much narration is telling you where it is unclear. The guide is not the problem — it is an accurate map of the problem. Every step is a screen that could not speak for itself.
Fix
Treat the 55 guide steps as a design backlog, not a feature. Each quarter, pick the ten most-triggered steps and redesign the screen until the step is unnecessary, then delete it. Target halving the count in two quarters. Separately, merge the three systems into one — they do the same job with three codebases.
Effort
Consolidation: 1 week. The backlog: ongoing, and it is the real design roadmap.
D2

Screens lead with machinery instead of content

Information design
Evidence
Four instances observed in one week, all shipped to production. The saved-places screen opened with a search box, two filter rows, a map card and five add buttons — roughly two screens of controls — above a vault containing one saved place. Trip cards printed 2026-09-17 – 2026-09-28 rather than a date a person would say. The world screen carried two permanent paragraphs of caveat under a counter reading zero. The globe drew city labels on top of each other, rendering both unreadable.
Reading
This is one failure with four faces: the screen is composed from the builder's point of view — here are the tools — rather than the user's — here is your stuff. Filters that cannot filter anything are not neutral; they push the content below the fold.
Fix
Adopt a standing rule: content first; a control appears only when it can act. The saved-places screen now implements exactly this — search appears at six items, filters when there is more than one value to filter by — so the pattern is proven in the codebase and needs generalising, not inventing. Audit every screen against it once.
Effort
2 weeks for a full sweep.
D3

No type scale, no spacing scale, no enforcement

System
Evidence
26 distinct font sizes, every one an arbitrary value. See the specimen above: 12px, 12.5px and 13px all ship, as do 14px, 14.5px and 15px, 15.5px, 16px and 16.5px.
Reading
Today this costs nothing, because one person decides every value and is consistent enough in practice. The bill arrives with the second designer, who has no way to know that 14.5px is body text and 14px is a mistake.
Fix
Name six or seven steps in the theme — caption, small, body, lead, title, display — map the existing 26 onto them, and make an arbitrary size a lint error. Do the same for spacing. This is a two-day job now and a two-month job after three more hires.
Effort
2–3 days, mostly mechanical.

Findings · material

Fix before the team grows

D4

A second design language sits unused in the repo

System
Evidence
46 components from a generic UI kit, 4,355 lines. Exactly four are used anywhere: a dialog, a calendar, a sheet, a switch. The other 42 — buttons, cards, inputs, badges, forms — are dead, and they look nothing like Béa.
Reading
A new designer or engineer opening components/ui/ will reasonably conclude that is the design system, and build against a language the product abandoned.
Fix
Delete the 42 unused files and keep the four in a clearly-named folder such as components/vendor/. Decide explicitly: Béa's own components are the system.
Effort
Half a day.
D5

The surface vocabulary is bypassed more often than used

System
Evidence
Named surfaces exist and are well-documented in the stylesheet — card-soft (64 uses), surface (25), card-raised (1). Raw background classes are used nearly twice as often: bg-card 160, bg-elevated 82.
Reading
The semantic layer is decorative rather than load-bearing, so elevation stops meaning anything. This produced a real bug fixed this week: a cream panel inside a cream panel inside a cream card, which read as one undifferentiated block with text floating in it.
Fix
Three surfaces, named by role — page, panel, floating — and a lint rule banning raw background utilities outside the theme file. Elevation should be a decision, not a class someone reached for.
Effort
3 days.
D6

Four ways to destroy something, and the worst one guards the worst action

Interaction
Evidence
Deletion is handled by an undo toast (8 sites), a styled dialog (89), a native browser confirm() (2), and four bare deletes with no protection at all. The two native confirms guard leaving a trip and removing a member — among the least reversible things a user can do here, handed to an unstyled system dialog in a system font.
Reading
Users learn safety from consistency. When the same gesture is sometimes undoable, sometimes confirmed, and sometimes instant, they learn to be afraid of all of it.
Fix
Two patterns, no exceptions. Reversible → do it immediately with an undo toast (the mechanism already exists and works). Irreversible → a styled confirm naming the consequence. Replace both native dialogs first; they are the highest-stakes and the cheapest to fix.
Effort
2 days.
D7

Touch targets below the accessible minimum

Accessibility
Evidence
Interactive elements at 36px (8 uses), 28px (9) and 20px (10) against a 44px minimum from both Apple's guidelines and WCAG 2.5.8. This is a phone-first product.
Reading
Small targets fail hardest for the users who are already struggling — in motion, one-handed, older, or with a tremor. It also reads as imprecision even to users who hit them.
Fix
Keep the visual size; expand the hit area with padding or a transparent overlay to 44px. The globe already does this — an invisible 32px circle behind a 6px pin — so the pattern is established and needs applying to the icon buttons.
Effort
2 days.
D8

Most screens have no designed empty state

Onboarding
Evidence
Four screens have written empty states — globe, home, saved places, trips. Photos, memories, story, expenses, calendar, packing, documents and to-dos have none or ad-hoc text.
Reading
Every new user sees the empty state before they see anything else. In a product whose entire value comes from accumulated personal data, the empty state is the onboarding — and it is doing more work than the 1,464-line tour ever will.
Fix
One template, applied everywhere: what this screen is for, in Béa's voice; one primary action; nothing else. The four that exist are good and set the standard.
Effort
1 week.

Findings · minor

Tickets, not discussions

D9–D11

Three smaller items

Low
  • The navigation is deeper than it looks. Six tabs, but 21 routes — fifteen screens reachable only from inside another screen. Expenses, calendar, story and memories have no fixed home. Worth a card-sort before adding anything else.
  • Loading is inconsistent. 28 loading flags, 15 skeleton references, and several screens that simply show nothing while fetching. Pick one: skeletons that match the shape of the content that is coming.
  • Motion is barely used and barely governed. Four references to reduced-motion across the app. Not a problem today because there is little animation, but establish the rule before someone adds a page transition.

The asset

What would be expensive to replace

These are the parts that make me say asset rather than liability. Most seed-stage products have the opposite profile — a tidy system with nothing to say.

✓ The voice is real and written down

A positioning document, a voice module with six defined modes, and a signature line per surface. The product refuses to call itself an AI travel planner, in writing, and the copy holds that line everywhere.

✓ The copy has a character

"Past You left a breadcrumb." "Choosing is harder than dreaming." "Professionally assembled from your own excellent ideas." Distinctive, consistent, and doing product work rather than decoration.

✓ The colour work is sophisticated

Built in oklch rather than hex or hsl, so lightness is perceptually even across hues — a choice most teams make years later, if ever. 141 tokens with complete dark-mode coverage.

✓ Undo exists

A working undo mechanism for removals, including the careful case: when a delete fails, it declines to offer undo rather than duplicating the row. Most products at this stage have no undo at all.

✓ Accessibility has been started, not ignored

99 aria-labels, 35 explicit roles, 22 aria-expanded, 11 aria-pressed, screen-reader-only text where icons carry meaning. Incomplete, but the intent is present in the code.

✓ The team responds to real evidence

Eight interface problems were found and fixed in a single week, each traced to an observed failure rather than a preference. That responsiveness is worth more than any individual screen.

Plan

One quarter to a system

Weeks 1–2

Stop the drift

  • Name the type and spacing scales; lint arbitrary values (D3).
  • Three named surfaces; ban raw background classes (D5).
  • Delete the 42 unused kit components (D4).
  • Replace both native confirm dialogs (D6).
Weeks 3–6

Make the screens speak

  • Content-first audit across every screen (D2).
  • Empty state on every screen, one template (D8).
  • Touch targets to 44px throughout (D7).
  • Merge three onboarding systems into one (D1).
Weeks 7–12

Turn the tour into a backlog

  • Redesign the ten most-used guide steps out of existence (D1).
  • Card-sort the navigation before adding features (D9).
  • Publish the system so a second designer can join without a briefing.

How this review was conducted, and its limits

This is a review of source, stylesheets and rendered output at commit b7d30f2, plus screenshots of the running app on an iPad and a phone. Counts are measured from the codebase, not estimated.

Three things it does not cover, and which should inform any decision as much as the above: no usability testing with real users, no analytics on where people actually struggle — none is collected today — and no review of the signed-in experience with a populated account, which I could not access. Findings D1, D2 and D8 in particular would be sharpened considerably by watching five people use the app for twenty minutes.

Prepared as an independent design assessment, to be read with the engineering audit of the same commit. Severity reflects cost to users and to future team velocity, not visual preference. Effort assumes one designer working with one engineer.
